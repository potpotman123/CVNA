## 1. Jefferies: operating data (05, pp. 1–6)

### Sales inferred from website listings

Estimated retail-unit growth was 35% in July, 41% in August and approximately 40% in the first three weeks of September. The first three September weeks individually showed 49%, 38% and 33% growth. Assuming historical seasonality for the remaining days implied Q3 sales of 216,548, up 39%, versus consensus 205,837: about 5% upside. Its actual model used a more conservative 210,541, up 35%.

Method: Sequentum collects daily inventory by stock number. A disappearing listing initially counts as a sale. Vehicles can reappear; estimates are revised using historical discrepancies. These are inferred transactions, not completed-sale records. Relistings, cancellations and other inventory movements matter.

### Inventory flow and speed

- Estimated average daily website additions: 2,531 in Q3 versus 2,347 in Q2.
- Average days to sell: 11.7 in Q3 versus 11.1 in Q2 and 13.3 a year earlier.
- Website inventory growth approximately 30% since June versus approximately 25% in the first five months.
- September 19 inventory estimate: 82,369; the quarter-end projection uses this observation.

Website selling time excludes the full purchase/reconditioning/delivery cash cycle. Read inventory additions, inventory stock and inferred exits together.

### Prices and mix

Estimated Q3 transaction price $27,626, up approximately 5% YoY; September listing prices up high single digits. Inventory was younger and lower-mileage YoY. This supports a mix explanation for some price increases, not necessarily greater like-for-like pricing power. Scraped vehicle prices differ from reported revenue per unit.

Jefferies proposed three Q3 megasite openings, versus three during all of H1, and productivity software as explanations for inventory availability. It did not quantify causal contributions.

### Hiring and traffic

LinkUp active job postings averaged 2,462 in June, 2,200 in July and 1,967 in August. August growth was only 1% YoY, far below vehicle growth. Possible explanations include staffing already in place, hiring completion, productivity or changed recruiting. Postings are neither employment nor capacity.

SimilarWeb estimated strong website traffic growth; this is not company-reported traffic and is shared with other analysts' source sets.

### Forecast structure

|---|---:|---:|
| 2026 | 812,781 | 36.2% |
| 2027 | 1,032,781 | 27.1% |
| 2028 | 1,277,781 | 23.7% |

Each 2027 quarter adds 55,000 vehicles YoY: a simple absolute-growth assumption, not independent causal estimates by quarter. 2027 EBITDA forecast $3.997bn. Base valuation $88, approximately 26x 2027 EBITDA; bull $140 and bear $35 are scenarios. Model also separates ADESA marketplace volumes: 2026 forecast 896,575, down 10.9%, despite Carvana retail growth.

## 2. Morgan Stanley September: capacity and operating leverage (07)

### Regional production versus sales (pp. 2–3)

Company data reproduced in the report:

| Q2 regional group | Production growth | Retail-unit growth |
|---|---:|---:|
| Midwest/Northeast | 57% | 54% |
| Mid-Atlantic/Texas-South | 39% | 34% |
| Southeast/West | 17% | 30% |

MS interprets this as supply-constrained growth: local availability improves selection and delivery convenience. Correlation does not establish causality; capacity may have been allocated toward stronger demand.

### Reconditioning bottlenecks (p. 4)

Specific levers: recruit/train staff, open production lines, deploy tools, optimize vehicle flow and reduce labor hours per car. Main competing interpretations: temporary growing pains versus costs that recur with rapid growth.

### Capacity investment (p. 7)

- Earlier limited ADESA integrations: approximately $2–3m/site.
- Full ADESA buildouts: approximately $30–35m/site.
- MS estimate for greenfield IRC: $45–80m.
- 20 integrated ADESA sites at report date, out of 56; company guidance 6–8 integrations in 2026.
- MS expects 3m annual retail units in 2032, within management's 2030–2035 target window.

A site opening is not a standardized amount of capacity or capex.

Illustrative IRC model: 60,000 annual capacity, 35% initial/85% mature utilization, $6,100 gross profit including finance and $1,620 operating costs per vehicle. Outputs: $94m first-year/$228m mature operating profit. These attribute downstream economics to the facility and are not demonstrated fully reconciled company ROIC; inventory funding and the rest of the network require consideration.

MS estimates blended capacity investment of approximately $1.46bn per additional million annual vehicles. ADESA calculation includes $2.2bn acquisition and $1.5bn integration costs; greenfield-only estimate approximately $1bn/million. Denominators and acquired assets differ.

### Long-term operating leverage (p. 6)

Total GPU approximately flat: $7,026 in 2025 to $6,993 in 2030. Adjusted EBITDA/unit rises from $3,749 to $4,435, principally through $900+ improvement in SG&A/unit. This thesis requires overhead growing more slowly than units, not continued GPU expansion.

### Credit and customer evidence (pp. 5, 9–16)

Intex charts: recent prime vintages better than 2023; recent subprime default vintages better than some earlier vintages. Counterevidence: subprime 60+ day delinquencies high relative to peers; Carvana/CarMax prime delinquency and default performance weaker than many other issuer pools. Compare borrower mix and vintage age, not headline pool labels alone.

Morning Consult NPS is weaker among younger/lower-income respondents; NPS declined for both brands before a recent inflection. It is a different survey from October AlphaWise and should not be conflated with it.

New-car dealership acquisitions and autonomous-fleet management are proposed adjacencies. September bull valuation includes $8/share for autonomous-fleet optionality; this is speculative, not current earnings.

## 7. JPMorgan: financing, marketplaces and supply (08)

### Pricing transparency (pp. 3, 8)

JPM attributes dealer advertised-price increases to FTC-related fee transparency. Initial implied fee inclusion $225; broader estimated pass-through $371. Estimated relative benefit CVNA $371/KMX $376. Mechanism: already-transparent prices become more competitive when rivals include formerly excluded charges; benefit can go to unit growth or pricing.

This is analyst causal attribution, not independently verified regulation or impact. JPM attributes half KMX comp growth to this effect; WB describes conversion/pricing/service improvements. They overlap and cannot be added.

### Loan penetration and margins (p. 12)

Estimated penetration 70% in 2022, 80% 2023, 84% 2024, 86% 2025, 83% 2027 forecast. These are JPM estimates.

| Metric | 2025 | 2026E | 2027E |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Gain/principal sold | 9.7% | 7.3% | 6.9% |
| Loan gain/all retail units | $2,000 | $1,701 | $1,543 |
| Finance profit/all retail units | $2,093 | $1,783 | $1,639 |

Finance profit includes interest earned before sale. Denominator includes buyers using cash/outside lenders. Principal sold/originations approximately 97% in 2025 and forecast 98% in 2026–27: originations are monetized, not all accumulated indefinitely.

2025 channel margin estimates: securitizations 9.1%; forward-flow/fixed-pool 9.9%. Private-channel profitability is partly inferred, not directly observable transaction-by-transaction.

### Rate sensitivities and repricing (pp. 3, 12–16)

Rule of thumb: 25bp compression in borrower APR minus funding cost → about 50bp less loan-sale margin → approximately $100 lower GPU. Customer-rate repricing described as lagging benchmarks three to six months. Keeping APRs low can protect conversion/penetration at a cost to margin.

Prime ABS average APR approximately 330bp lower than Q1 2025. Prime-labeled pools contain approximately 30% nonprime loans, so labels do not establish identical risk to other issuers.

Illustrative prime deal: $22,500 loan, 10.8% APR, 5.1% funding coupon, 1% servicing fee, 3% lifetime net losses, 2.2-year weighted-average life. Gain $1,356; retained interest $1,463; immediate proceeds $22,393 = principal + gain − retained asset. Positive gain need not mean immediate cash exceeds principal.

Stress illustration: prime gain $1,356 → $1,252, subprime $1,731 → $629; lifetime loss assumptions 3% → 4% prime and 20% → 25% subprime. Selected assumptions, not comprehensive stress bounds. Some recession-table comments do not match displayed numerical changes.

### Partner marketplace (p. 10)

Illustrative per-unit GP: finance/service contracts $2,900; merchandising $500; fulfillment/customer service $400; shipping $500; reconditioning profit $500. Total $4,800; incremental SG&A $1,450; incremental EBITDA $3,350. Not disclosed actual fee contracts.

### Consumer sourcing (p. 19)

### Off-lease supply (pp. 20–21)

Separate p.20 supply table has materially different assumptions/availability estimates. Reconcile before adopting either series; some displayed formulas also require checking.

## 8. BofA: AI and valuation counterarguments (01, pp. 1–2)

AI risks: outside-financing shopping lowers take-up; competitive APR pressure narrows spreads; prime departures alter credit mix; refinancing accelerates prepayments and reduces residual value.

Counterarguments: down payment, approval, convenience, integrated trade-in/purchase/delivery matter alongside APR. Nonprime can have higher per-unit economics from wider spreads, but is not therefore safer.

Illustration: +94bp APR on $25k/72-month loan adds approximately $11/month. BofA expects more effect on near-term financing profits than units; example is not a demand-elasticity estimate. Elevated third-party unit data cited through investor conversations, not independent BofA measurement.

$70 valuation assumes 17% unit CAGR through 2032, 19% gross margin, SG&A 6% revenue and 14% WACC. Contrast MS 12% WACC and much larger long-run shares. Targets depend heavily on growth/discounting, not just quarterly forecast differences.
